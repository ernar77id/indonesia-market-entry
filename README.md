# Indonesia Banking & Fintech Market Entry Snapshot

*As at July 2026. Indonesian financial regulation is in a fast reform cycle - every figure below carries that date.*

**Currency note:** Figures use USD/IDR of about 17,950 (spot, early July 2026 - the rupiah has weakened notably over the past year). IDR 1 billion is about USD 56k; IDR 1 trillion about USD 56m.

> **Health warning.** Indonesian financial regulation is in an unusually fast reform cycle (the 2023 "P2SK" omnibus financial-sector law is still being implemented through dozens of new OJK/BI regulations). Every capital figure and ownership cap below should be re-confirmed with local counsel at the point of a live transaction. Where a number is known to be under active revision, it is flagged.

---

## 1. Summary

**Financial depth is shallow - which is the entire opportunity.** Formal **bank-account ownership** sits at only about 50-65% of adults (broader financial inclusion, counting e-money accounts, reaches about 75-85%), the retail base skews to basic savings accounts rather than full current/transaction accounts, and **credit-card penetration is just about 6-8%** - essentially flat for a decade, held down by Bank Indonesia's strict income and credit-history thresholds. That gap has been filled not by banks but by **digital wallets - now the dominant payment rail, used by about 96% of digital-payment users, a base well past 100 million** (GoPay, OVO, DANA, ShopeePay) - and by BNPL/paylater. The pattern is decisive: **payments have leapfrogged to mobile, but deposits, formal credit, and account ownership remain low**.

The market this happens in is enormous: about 284 million people, about 79% internet penetration, a digital-economy GMV approaching **USD 100 billion** in 2025, of which e-commerce is roughly **USD 71-94 billion** - Southeast Asia's largest digital economy and the single most attractive scale market in the region. It is also the most contested and the most heavily regulated.

**Two regulators split the perimeter:**

- **OJK** (Otoritas Jasa Keuangan / Financial Services Authority) - banks, rural banks (BPR), multifinance, P2P lending (now "LPBBTI"), insurance, capital markets, and financial-sector tech innovation (ITSK). As of Jan 2025, OJK also took over crypto assets from Bappebti.
- **Bank Indonesia (BI)** - payment systems, e-money, QRIS, settlement rails, and macro/FX.

**The central strategic fact:** a full commercial-bank licence is now prohibitively expensive - roughly **IDR 10 trillion (about USD 557m) paid-up capital to establish a new bank**, and existing banks must hold **IDR 3 trillion (about USD 167m) minimum core capital**. This has pushed almost every recent entrant (Jago, Superbank, SeaBank, Krom) to **buy** an existing bank rather than build one - and it makes an **acquire-and-combine** route through the cheaper licence tiers the rational, capital-efficient entry. Which licences combine, in what order, and why is the subject of section 8.

**What is genuinely attractive:**

- Enormous underbanked/thin-file population; about 80% of MSMEs are underserved by incumbent retail banks.
- **Household debt is only about 15% of GDP (about USD 213bn)** - among the lowest in Asia (Thailand about 85-90%, Malaysia about 67-70%, global median about 57%) - meaning enormous headroom for credit growth without systemic-leverage risk, and a regulator that can afford to stay growth-friendly toward productive lending.
- A mature identity and e-signature stack (Dukcapil e-KTP, certified PSrE e-signatures) that makes fully digital onboarding legally robust - rare in emerging markets.
- Clear, tiered, activity-based licensing with low capital thresholds (P2P at IDR 25bn, about USD 1.4m; BPR in the low tens of billions of IDR via acquisition, while new establishment now runs IDR 25-100bn; multifinance licences typically held at IDR 50-100bn equity, about USD 2.8-5.6m, recapitalised toward IDR 250bn, about USD 14m, on acquisition), though the cheapest of these must now typically be **acquired rather than newly licensed** (moratoria/consolidation across P2P, multifinance, and banks).
- Room to add an **embedded-finance / BaaS** layer later (still relatively white space, especially on a BPR base) once own-brand products are proven.

**What is genuinely hard:**

- **Foreign ownership and domestic-control rules** differ by licence and are the single biggest structuring constraint (see each section). BPR foreign ownership in particular is legally unsettled.
- Payments and marketplaces are effectively **won** by entrenched incumbents (GoPay/OVO/DANA/ShopeePay; Shopee/TikTok-Tokopedia). Competing head-on is value-destructive; the opportunity is the **rails and credit layer beneath them**.
- OJK is in a **consolidation and clean-up phase** (post-Investree fraud, BPR failures), so scrutiny of new owners and fit-and-proper testing is heavy.
- A **strict new data-protection law (PDP, GDPR-style)**, data-residency expectations, and a labour regime that swung **back toward worker protection** in late 2024.

**Bottom line:** the market rewards a *licensed, data-advantaged, low-cost-to-serve* operator that lends through trusted anchors rather than competing for consumers head-on, then funds the book cheaply once it's proven. Section 8 outlines a group built to that specification.

## 2. Lending

Indonesia offers a laddered set of credit vehicles. The trade-off is always **capital + regulatory burden vs. breadth of activity**.

### 2.1 Comparison of lending vehicles

| Vehicle | Min. capital (entry) | Can take public deposits? | Payments/FX? | Max foreign ownership | Governing rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Commercial Bank** (Bank Umum) | about IDR 10tn (about USD 557m) to establish new; IDR 3tn (about USD 167m) core capital to operate | Yes (full) | Yes | Up to **99%** | POJK 12/2021; Law 4/2023 (P2SK) |
| **Digital Bank** | Same as commercial bank (not a separate licence) | Yes | Yes | Up to 99% | POJK 12/2021 + POJK 21/2023 (digital services) |
| **Rural Bank** (BPR / BPRS) | Zone-based: **IDR 25-100bn** (about USD 1.4-5.6m) for new establishment under POJK 7/2024 (BPRS 15-75bn; historically IDR 4-14bn) | Savings & time deposits only (**no demand/checking**) | **No direct payments; no FX** | **Unsettled - see 2.3** | POJK 7/2024 |
| **Multifinance** (Perusahaan Pembiayaan) | **IDR 250bn** (about USD 14m) for *new* establishment; existing hold about IDR 50-100bn (about USD 2.8-5.6m) equity (floor IDR 100bn) - acquirers recap toward 250bn | No | No | Up to **85%** | POJK 47/2020; POJK 46/2024 |
| **P2P / LPBBTI** ("pindar") | **IDR 25bn** (about USD 1.4m) paid-up | No (intermediary only) | No | Up to **85%** | POJK 40/2024 |
| **Securities Crowdfunding** | Lower; capital-markets rule | No | No | Restricted | POJK 17/2025 |

**On- vs off-balance-sheet:** a **multifinance lends on its own balance sheet** - it funds the loans and holds them, keeping the full spread but consuming its own capital. A **P2P/LPBBTI platform lends off balance sheet** - it only matches third-party lenders to borrowers and is legally barred from lending its own book (POJK 40/2024), earning fees/spread without holding the loans. This distinction drives how each is funded and capitalised (see §8.2 and §8.10).

### 2.2 Commercial and digital banks

A "digital bank" is **not a separate licence** in Indonesia - it is a commercial bank that operates predominantly digitally (POJK 12/2021; digital services under POJK 21/2023). It therefore carries the full commercial-bank capital load (IDR 3tn core / IDR 10tn to establish new - about USD 167m / about USD 557m). Banks are tiered into **KBMI 1-4** by core capital, which drives the range of activities permitted. Foreign ownership can reach 99%, but new licences are essentially closed in practice - the realistic path is acquiring an existing small bank and recapitalising, which is what every recent digital-bank entrant did. **This is capital-out of scope for an efficient first entry**, but a credible eventual conversion target for an established group (see section 8).

**Insider view:** obtaining a *new* commercial or digital-bank licence is **effectively impossible** - OJK has not been granting greenfield bank licences, and the about IDR 10tn (about USD 557m) establishment bar deters entry on its own. The only viable route into full banking is to **acquire an existing commercial or digital bank and recapitalise/re-platform it** - the playbook behind Bank Jago, Superbank, SeaBank, and Krom.

### 2.3 BPR (Bank Perekonomian Rakyat)

The **Bank Perekonomian Rakyat** (People's Economy Bank; renamed from *Bank Perkreditan Rakyat* under the P2SK Law) is the cheapest way to hold a genuine, deposit-taking, credit-issuing **banking** licence.

**What a BPR can do:** collect savings and time deposits, extend credit/financing (incl. sharia), place funds interbank, and - new under POJK 7/2024 - **go public** (issue equity/debt on the IDX) and merge/consolidate.

**Hard limits (these define what a BPR can and cannot be used for):**
- **Cannot provide payment-transaction services directly** - this is *why* a BPR must be paired with a payment partner (Section 3). Concretely: a BPR **cannot open current accounts (giro)** - funding is savings and time deposits only - and **cannot issue credit cards**; it **can issue ATM cards** on savings accounts via network cooperation (ATM Bersama/PRIMA, typically through a partner bank), while full merchant-debit cards remain a payment-system product requiring BI approvals. Post-P2SK, BPRs may now conduct **fund transfers and FX money-changing**, and can access BI-FAST through cooperation with a commercial bank (not yet as direct participants).
- **No foreign exchange** business beyond the money-changing activity above.
- **Geographically constrained** and zone-based; OJK actively steers BPRs toward their assigned regions and toward consolidation.
- Zone-based minimum paid-up capital for NEW establishment: **IDR 100bn / 50bn / 25bn by zone** under POJK 7/2024 (BPRS 75/35/15bn), up from IDR 4-14bn (about USD 220k-780k) under the prior regime - one more reason acquisition beats establishment. BPRs with at least **IDR 50bn** (about USD 2.8m) core capital face heavier governance/compliance-unit obligations.

**Foreign-ownership caveat (critical):** the P2SK Law *removed* the old requirement that a BPR be "wholly owned by Indonesian nationals," and POJK 7/2024 implements the change, allowing ownership by "Indonesian legal entities," which arguably includes an Indonesian PMA (foreign-investment company). **This has opened a door but is not settled** - market practice, OJK approval appetite, and structuring (e.g., a PMA holding company, or JV with a local partner) must be tested before committing. Treat BPR foreign control as the **single largest legal risk** in any structure that includes one.

**Insider view:** the BPR sector is fragmented (about 1,500+ BPRs), under-digitised, and being culled by OJK for weak governance. That is exactly the condition in which a **well-capitalised, AI-native, professionally-run acquirer** can roll up or upgrade a BPR licence cheaply and differentiate massively on technology. Komunal and others have shown BPR deposits can be aggregated digitally. Three practical points from current OJK practice:

- **The foreign-acquisition window is open but narrow.** Several BPRs are reported to be under OJK review for acquisition by foreign parties. OJK's practice is to approve only a **limited number of such deals and then pause further approvals** - so timing matters. A viable target and application should be secured early rather than assumed to be available later.
- **One BPR is enough - no regional roll-up is required.** A single BPR is geographically confined for *its own* lending, but in a combined group **national lending can be booked on a non-bank lending licence** (§8.2), so the BPR's zone limits need not constrain a group's credit reach - no multi-BPR roll-up is needed for lending. A multi-BPR roll-up (e.g., an East Java BPR merged with BPRs in Bali and North Sumatra) remains a *possible later* way to widen the deposit-gathering and branch footprint, but it is **not required** for the lending model and adds cost and integration risk. Additional BPRs are optional, not a requirement of the model.
- **"Public offering" comes in three tiers - and they differ enormously in weight.** POJK 7/2024 now lets a BPR go public, and in practice a raise falls into three tiers of increasing weight. (i) A **product/project raise via securities crowdfunding (SCF)** - under POJK 17/2025 (which replaced POJK 57/2020 in July 2025), this is *not* treated as a full public offering if it raises **at most IDR 10bn (about USD 557k) within 12 months** to fund a specific project and the issuer stays private (under 300 shareholders and under IDR 30bn (about USD 1.7m) paid-up capital); entry tickets are small and platform-set (reportedly from about **IDR 500m** (about USD 28k) - treat as a platform practice, not a regulatory floor, and confirm). (ii) An **expansion raise** - larger growth capital via private placement or a small/SME offering, above the SCF cap but short of a main-board listing. (iii) A **full ("real") IPO** - a Penawaran Umum with IDX listing, full prospectus, audited accounts, and OJK effectiveness, the heaviest route. The rational choice is the lightest route that meets the capital need.

### 2.4 Multifinance

**IDR 250bn (about USD 14m) minimum paid-up capital applies to establishing a *new* finance company** (POJK 47/2020); existing licences operate under the **IDR 100bn (about USD 5.6m) minimum-equity floor** and in practice typically hold equity of only **about IDR 50-100bn (about USD 2.8-5.6m)**. On an acquisition, OJK's practice is to expect the incoming owner to **recapitalise toward the IDR 250bn new-entrant standard** - i.e. the capital plan is the purchase price *plus the recap difference*, not IDR 250bn on top (OJK can ask for more, but within reason). Up to 85% foreign. Its permitted activities (POJK 35/2018) are **investment financing, working-capital financing, and multipurpose/consumer financing** - in practice spanning leasing, instalment purchase, cash facilities, and increasingly BNPL/paylater. (Factoring/*anjak piutang* is legally included under working-capital financing, but as a product it is marginal in the Indonesian market.) It **lends on its own balance sheet**, but **cannot take deposits**, so it must fund itself (equity, bank lines, bonds, joint financing).

**Insider view:** OJK is in a **consolidation phase and in practice not issuing new multifinance licences** (a market reality rather than a codified moratorium), so acquiring an existing licence is the realistic route. Two points make the licence more valuable than it first looks:

- **BNPL is now a bank-or-multifinance-only product.** Under **POJK 32/2025** (effective 15 December 2025), "buy-now-pay-later" as a distinct financing product may be offered *only* by commercial banks (*bank umum*) and multifinance companies (finance companies need prior OJK approval). **P2P platforms, e-wallets, and - importantly - BPRs cannot offer BNPL directly** (a BPR is not a *bank umum*, and BNPL also runs through a point-of-sale payment flow a BPR can't operate), and e-commerce players can only participate by partnering with a bank or multifinance. A multifinance licence is therefore one of just two ways to *own* BNPL - the fastest-growing consumer-credit product in Indonesia (multifinance BNPL outstanding grew about 70% year-on-year in late 2025).
- **On paper broad, in practice narrow - and credit cards are effectively bank-only.** A multifinance licence formally covers investment, working-capital, and multipurpose financing under **POJK 35/2018** (including cash/multipurpose facilities up to IDR 500m (about USD 28k) per debtor), but in practice these firms concentrate on **leasing, asset-collateral financing (chiefly vehicle and equipment financing secured by fiduciary title / *jaminan fidusia*), and instalment/long-term consumer lending**. Credit-card issuance is treated as bank territory: the **first and only widely cited non-bank issuer is AEON Credit Service Indonesia** (part of Japan's AEON group), issuing the AEON Card since 2013 - a legacy approval, not a repeatable route.

### 2.5 P2P lending / LPBBTI ("pindar")

The lowest-capital regulated credit licence: **IDR 25bn (about USD 1.4m) paid-up** (POJK 40/2024), plus a **50%-of-paid-up equity ratio**, mandatory **credit-scoring** on every disbursement, **UBO disclosure**, and a **max funding of IDR 2bn (about USD 111k) per borrower** (up to **IDR 5bn (about USD 279k) for productive/business loans**, subject to platform-health conditions). A P2P platform lends **off balance sheet** - it matches third-party lenders to borrowers and is **barred from lending its own book**, earning fees/spread rather than holding the loans. That makes it a **capital-light** way to scale lending with other people's money (its main strategic use - see §8.10), at the cost of not owning the asset. Foreign ownership up to 85% (implementing rules pending). It may now run **conventional + sharia in one entity** and be owned by a cooperative.

**Insider view:** P2P (LPBBTI) carries the lowest capital (IDR 25bn, about USD 1.4m) - **but the sector is under heavy supervisory pressure and is not a greenfield option**:

- **No new licences - acquisition only.** OJK introduced a **moratorium on new P2P licences in 2020**, so the only way in is to buy an existing platform - and confirm the target is clear of the **3-year post-licence lock-up** on ownership/control changes.
- **Active consolidation.** Over the past few years OJK has pushed licensed platforms **below 100** (from a much larger pool of operators at the 2019-2020 peak) and wants the number to keep falling; the Investree collapse and enforcement actions have hardened supervisory attitudes.
- **Rate caps ratcheted down.** The all-in daily cost cap has fallen from the about 0.8%/day era (and far higher among illegal apps) to **0.1%/day for consumptive loans from 1 January 2026** (productive funding is capped separately), with total charges - interest, fees and penalties combined - capped at **100% of principal** (SEOJK 19/2025).
- **Productive over consumptive.** OJK is steering the sector toward **SME/productive lending**: productive funding can reach **IDR 5bn** (about USD 279k) per borrower (vs the **IDR 2bn** (about USD 111k) base cap), while consumptive loans face a tighter repayment-capacity limit (borrower obligations at most **30% of income** from 2026, down from 40%). Ultra-short "payday"-style single-repayment lending is being squeezed out; instalment structures remain acceptable.
- **Funding side moving institutional.** OJK is pushing platforms toward professional/institutional and bank funding - retail ("non-professional") lenders are being capped to **20% of a platform's outstanding funding** (from 2027) - steadily narrowing the old crowd-funded, retail-lender model.

The upshot on data: P2P runs its own **real-time** shared system - **AFPI's Fintech Data Center (FDC)**, plus OJK's supervisory **Pusdafil** - which every licensed platform must feed daily and query before lending to check cross-platform exposure and default history (and enforce the three-platform borrower limit). FDC is an **exposure/negative-list check, not a full credit bureau**. Historically P2P data sat outside the national bureau (SLIK), but **POJK 11/2024 now requires P2P lenders to report to and participate in SLIK (from 31 July 2025)** - so a licensed platform gains SLIK access, while alternative data (telco, e-commerce, cash-flow) still isn't in any bureau, which is where an AI-native group's edge lies. In the section 8 design, a P2P licence is an **optional later lever** (§8.10) - acquired, not built, and not a core piece.

## 3. Payments

Payments are regulated by **Bank Indonesia**. The **activity- and risk-based** regime (not entity-based) was built by the 2020 "umbrella" framework (PBI 22/23/2020; PBI 23/6/2021 for PJP; PBI 23/7/2021 for PIP) and consolidated by **PBI 10/2025 (effective 31 March 2026)**, which revoked the 2020 umbrella and carries the licence categories and capital tiers below forward, with a multi-year transition.

### 3.1 Licence types and capital

| Licence | Scope | Min. initial capital |
|---|---|---|
| **PJP Category 1** | Full: account information + payment initiation/acquiring + account issuance (source-of-funds admin) + remittance | **IDR 15bn** (about USD 835k) |
| **PJP Category 2** | Payment initiation/acquiring + account issuance | **IDR 5bn** (about USD 280k) |
| **PJP Category 3** | Narrow (e.g., remittance / limited) | **IDR 500m** (about USD 28k), or **IDR 1bn** (about USD 56k) if it provides a system usable by other Cat-3 PJPs |
| **PIP** (infrastructure operator - clearing/settlement) | Switching, clearing, settlement rails | **IDR 100bn** (about USD 5.6m); by BI *appointment*, not open application |

The four PJP activities are: (1) account-information services, (2) payment initiation & acquiring, (3) account issuance / source-of-funds administration (this is where **e-money / e-wallet** sits), and (4) remittance.

### 3.2 Ownership and control

- **Non-bank PJP:** foreign ownership capped at **85%**, **but** at least **51% of voting shares** and 15% of shares must be domestically held, and veto/board-nomination rights must sit with local shareholders. In practice BI wants **effective domestic control**.
- **PIP:** minimum **80% domestic** (max 20% foreign).
- Registered e-money account balance cap raised to **IDR 20m** (about USD 1,100).

### 3.3 Insider view

- BI licensing is **slow and selective**; in practice a **new licence is very hard to obtain - Categories 1-2 are close to unobtainable for a fresh entrant**, and market participants report that even a **Category 3 licence can take 2-3 years** to secure. BI periodically pauses new e-money licences and prefers to convert/consolidate existing players.
- **QRIS** is the mandated national QR standard and **BI-FAST** the real-time rail; margins are compressed by regulator-set/low MDR, so standalone wallet economics are brutal.
- The consumer wallet market is **effectively decided** - GoPay, OVO, DANA, ShopeePay, LinkAja dominate. **Do not try to build another consumer wallet.**
- **The pragmatic move for a new entrant is to *partner* with (or take a stake in) an existing licensed PJP** for rails, disbursement, collection, and QRIS acceptance, rather than obtain a Category-1 licence cold. A PJP licence is worth pursuing only once volume justifies owning the economics - and even then the domestic-control rules bite. The payment layer is a *utility input*, not a profit centre in itself.

## 4. Marketplaces

### 4.1 Structure and market shares (2024-2025, GMV; Momentum Works)

| Platform | about Share of Indonesia e-commerce GMV | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Shopee** (Sea) | **about 46%** | Clear leader; strong logistics + ads flywheel |
| **Tokopedia + TikTok Shop** (combined) | **about 34-38%** (Tokopedia about 23% + TikTok about 11%) | Merged after the 2023 social-commerce ban; live/video commerce engine |
| **Lazada** (Alibaba) | **about 6-7%** | Slipping; first monthly profit mid-2024 |
| **Blibli** | **about 3-4%** | Premium/electronics, omni-channel |
| **Bukalapak** | about 10% in 2024 to **exited physical-goods marketplace early 2025** | Pivoted to virtual products/O2O |

Total Indonesia e-commerce GMV about **USD 56.5bn (2024)**, projected **about USD 71-94bn (2025)**. Three platforms (Shopee, TikTok-Tokopedia, Lazada) held **98.8%** of ASEAN platform GMV in 2025 (84% in 2024).

### 4.2 Challenges

- **Near-oligopoly**: two ecosystems (Sea/Shopee and TikTok-Tokopedia) own the demand. New horizontal marketplaces have essentially no path.
- **Regulatory turbulence**: the 2023 ban on in-app social-commerce forced TikTok's Tokopedia acquisition; consumer-protection and predatory-discount rules keep shifting.
- **New tax drag**: from **14 July 2025**, qualifying marketplaces must withhold **0.5% income tax (PPh 22)** on domestic sellers' gross turnover (PMK 37/2025) - see Section 7.
- **Logistics and thin margins** outside Java.

### 4.3 Insider view

Do **not** enter as a marketplace. The strategically correct read is that **the marketplaces are the distribution, not the prize** - they sit on top of a payments-and-credit layer they largely rent. Every marketplace, social-commerce network, cooperative, and MSME aggregator is a potential **BaaS distribution partner** for embedded savings, working-capital credit, and merchant financing. The e-commerce section of this snapshot is best understood as a **map of demand aggregators the fintech group can serve**, not competitors to fight.

A specific opening: several e-commerce and tech players that earlier **built or acquired their own P2P or multifinance arms** have since found that lending is a fundamentally different business from commerce - capital-intensive, risk- and collections-heavy, and separately regulated. Some are now **more open to handing the financing business to a specialised third party** (via partnership, white-label, or an outright carve-out) than to keep running it in-house - a direct route both to distribution and, potentially, to a **ready-made licensed entity to acquire**.

## 5. Access to government data, identity, verification, KYC, e-signatures, credit data, collection

This is Indonesia's **quiet competitive advantage** and the technical backbone of any digital-lending model here.

### 5.1 Identity & KYC (Dukcapil / e-KTP)

- **Dukcapil** (Directorate General of Population & Civil Registration) holds the **NIK / e-KTP** national identity database, including the reference photo for **face-match**.
- Access is **not open**: it is intermediated. Regulated players reach Dukcapil either via an MoU (formal, slow) or, in practice, through **OJK-licensed "IKD Cluster" e-KYC providers** and **BI-registered fintech-support providers** - e.g., **ASLI RI, Privy, VIDA, Verihubs, Didit, uqudo**. These vendors offer NIK + liveness-selfie + Dukcapil face-match in **sub-30 seconds** via API. **ASLI RI** stands out for a lending-first group: direct Dukcapil biometric connectivity (face/fingerprint/iris), roots in the national-police security ecosystem, an installed base across banks and multifinance (endorsed by the APPI multifinance association), plus adjacent tooling - e-KYB (business verification), AI lending-acquisition risk profiling, and a collection/skip-tracing data product (§5.5).
- Being a **regulated financial institution** materially improves an entity's standing to obtain data access - another reason to hold a licence rather than operate as a pure tech vendor.

### 5.2 Digital / electronic signatures (PSrE)

- **Certified electronic signatures** carry the **same legal weight as wet ink** under the EIT/ITE Law (Art. 11) and GR 71/2019, if issued by a **Komdigi-recognised PSrE** (Certificate Authority) - e.g., Privy, VIDA, Peruri (state), Xignature.
- **POJK 40/2024 requires certified e-signatures for lending agreements** - so this is mandatory infrastructure, not optional.
- **e-Meterai** (electronic stamp duty, issued by Peruri) completes legally binding digital documents.
- Adoption is exploding (a single PSrE logged 32m+ signature transactions in Q1 2026, up about 250% YoY), driven by lending, paylater, banking, multifinance.

### 5.3 Credit data (SLIK + LPIP + alternative scoring)

- **SLIK** (OJK's central credit-information system, successor to "BI Checking") - **mandatory reporting** by all regulated banks, multifinance, and BPRs, and - **since 31 July 2025, under POJK 11/2024 - P2P lenders too** (previously they sat outside SLIK). Access to SLIK is a privilege of being a **regulated, reporting entity** - a decisive argument for holding a BPR/multifinance/P2P licence.
- **LPIP** - licensed private credit bureaus (e.g., **Pefindo Biro Kredit / IdScore, CLIK**) provide reports and scores, cross-referenced against Dukcapil.
- **Innovative Credit Scoring (ICS)** firms (alternative data) are now brought under OJK's ITSK framework.
- **P2P's data is being pulled into the fold.** P2P lending also runs its own **real-time** shared database - **AFPI's Fintech Data Center (FDC)**, which every licensed platform must feed daily and check before lending (cross-platform exposure, default history, the three-platform borrower cap) - plus OJK's supervisory **Pusdafil**. Historically this data sat *outside* SLIK; **POJK 11/2024 now brings P2P lenders into SLIK (from 31 July 2025)**, closing a long-standing silo - though FDC stays useful because SLIK is not real-time.
- **The gap = the opportunity:** even as P2P joins SLIK, **BNPL and alternative data remain fragmented and only partially captured**, and a large thin-file population still has no bureau history. This is exactly where **AI-native, alternative-data underwriting** (transaction, telco, e-commerce, cash-flow signals - with consent) creates a defensible edge.

### 5.4 Data protection & AML

- **PDP Law (Law 27/2022)** - Indonesia's GDPR-style regime; **full enforcement from October 2024**. Consent, data-subject rights, breach notification, cross-border-transfer controls, and **fines up to 2% of annual revenue**. A dedicated data-protection authority is being stood up.
- **Data-residency** expectations under the electronic-systems rules (GR 71/2019) push toward in-country hosting for financial data - factor into cloud/infra design.
- **AML/CFT:** PPATK (financial-intelligence unit), Law 8/2010, plus OJK/BI AML rules - full KYC/CDD, sanctions/PEP screening, and reporting obligations apply to every licensed entity.

**Insider view:** the identity + e-signature + bureau stack is good enough to run a **100% digital, legally enforceable** lending and deposit operation - but access is *permissioned and intermediated*, and PDP compliance is now real. A licence + a strong e-KYC/PSrE partner + AI underwriting on alternative data is the winning combination.

### 5.5 Collection - what recovery actually looks like

Origination is digital and clean; **recovery is the constrained end of the pipeline**. Collection conduct is governed primarily by **POJK 22/2023** (consumer protection), which applies to all licensed lenders (banks, BPRs, multifinance, P2P) and makes the lender **fully liable for its collectors' conduct, including outsourced third parties**. Three tiers in practice:

- **Soft collection (desk).** Reminders, calls, in-app notifications, restructuring/rescheduling offers. Contact is allowed **only with the borrower** - not employers, relatives, or emergency contacts -, **Monday-Saturday, 08.00-20.00 local time**, at the agreed address/domicile, with no threats, humiliation, physical/verbal pressure, or continuous harassing contact. This is where most recovery must happen - which is why **repayment-at-source design** (salary/member deduction, settlement share) matters more than collection capacity. For P2P platforms there is also a hard clock: under the AFPI code of conduct binding every licensed platform (SK Pengurus 02/2020, Annex III), **direct collection by the platform must stop once a loan is more than 90 days past due** - the loan is then classified non-performing (the sector's headline default metric, TWP90, counts exactly these loans), and any further recovery must run through a **certified, OJK/AFPI-recognised third-party collection agency** or the courts. The debt itself does not extinguish, and the default is reported to SLIK and FDC. Specialist vendors support this tier with contact-data refresh and skip-tracing tools (e.g. **ASLI RI**'s collection/data-search product, §5.1) - subject to PDP-law limits on data use.
- **Hard collection (field).** Visits and - for secured loans - repossession. Field collectors must be **certified** (by an OJK-registered certification body/association), carry an **assignment letter and ID**, and any third-party agency must be a **licensed legal entity**; the lender remains liable for their violations, with administrative fines up to **IDR 15bn (about USD 836k)** plus licence measures. For fiducia-secured assets, the Constitutional Court's fiducia ruling (Decision 18/PUU-XVII/2019) means **repossession requires the debtor's voluntary surrender or a court execution order** - unilateral seizure is no longer safe. Politically, the space is tightening: thousands of collection-conduct complaints reach OJK yearly and there are active legislative calls to ban third-party collection outright.
- **Legal collection (courts).** For claims up to **IDR 500m (about USD 28k)**, the **small-claims procedure** (*gugatan sederhana*, PERMA 2/2015 as amended by PERMA 4/2019) offers a decision in about 25 working days - useful against SME-sized defaults, though **execution** of a judgment is a separate, slower process. Above that: ordinary civil proceedings (years), fiducia execution through the court, or **PKPU/bankruptcy** petitions in the commercial court for larger corporate exposures - a credible threat point but slow and reputationally loaded. There is **no wage-garnishment regime** for consumer debt.

**Design implication (and why the section 8 design looks the way it does):** in Indonesia you don't build a collection machine and lend into the open market - you **design the loan so it repays itself** (deduction at payroll/coop source, settlement-share sweeps at the POS/payments layer) and keep soft collection as the main tool, hard and legal collection as narrow, compliant last resorts.

## 6. Labour regulation

### 6.1 Framework and recent swing

The **Job Creation Law (Law 6/2023, "UU Cipta Kerja")** liberalised labour rules to attract FDI - but **Constitutional Court Decision 168/PUU-XXI/2023 (issued 31 Oct 2024)** reversed much of that, **restoring worker protections** and ordering a **standalone Labour Law** (expected about 2026). Employers now operate under a more protective, still-transitioning regime.

### 6.2 Key rules

- **Contracts:** fixed-term (**PKWT**) capped at **5 years incl. extensions**, must be written in Bahasa Indonesia/Latin script; misuse auto-converts to permanent. Permanent (**PKWTT**) probation max **3 months**.
- **Severance:** three stacked components on termination - **Uang Pesangon** (severance, up to 9 months' wage), **Uang Penghargaan Masa Kerja** (long-service, up to 10 months), and **Uang Penggantian Hak** (accrued rights). Multipliers vary by termination ground; amounts are **minimums**. **Termination now generally requires going through the industrial-relations dispute process** - dismissal is slow and costly.
- **Outsourcing:** now **restricted to six supporting-service categories** under Permenaker 7/2026 (April 2026, implementing the Constitutional Court ruling) - no longer freely available.
- **Foreign workers:** an operational or technical expat hire needs a **Working KITAS**, an **RPTKA** (Foreign Worker Utilisation Plan; Minister of Manpower is sole approver), and a **DKP-TKA levy (about USD 100/month - USD 1,200/year - per worker, payable annually in advance)**. The main exemption is the **Investor KITAS (index E28A)**: a foreign individual named in the company's deed (*akta*) holding **at least IDR 10bn (about USD 557k) in personal shares** and serving as **director or commissioner** needs no RPTKA and pays no DKP-TKA - but that permit covers **only the governance role (setting strategy, signing, representing the company), not operational work**. A **non-shareholder** director/commissioner - or a shareholder below the threshold who wants to actually *work* - falls back to the Working-KITAS + RPTKA route. Employers must **prioritise Indonesians** and provide local-understudy/transfer plans. Expect to run a **majority-local team with a few key expat roles**.
- **Social security (BPJS):** mandatory enrolment in **BPJS Ketenagakerjaan** (accident, death, old-age, pension, job-loss) and **BPJS Kesehatan** (health); combined employer contributions about **10-11%+** of payroll.

### 6.3 Minimum wage (benchmark)

Set annually per province (UMP) and city (UMK) under the **new wage formula in PP 49/2025** (inflation + economic growth x alpha, alpha 0.5-0.9). **Jakarta UMP 2026 = IDR 5,729,876/month (about USD 320), +6.17%**; national average increase about 5-7%. West Java is the lowest (IDR 2,317,601, about USD 129) - for 2026 it undercut Central Java, the historical floor. Skilled tech/engineering salaries sit well above minimum but remain low by regional standards.

**Insider view:** wage cost is competitive, but **termination rigidity, mandatory severance, outsourcing limits, and expat-hiring friction** are real. Two payroll costs are easy to under-budget: **severance** (a standing liability, as above) and the **THR (*Tunjangan Hari Raya*)** - a *mandatory* religious-holiday allowance equal to **one month's wage** for staff with 12+ months' service (pro-rated below that), payable **at least seven days before the employee's religious holiday**, with penalties (up to **5% of the amount due**) for late payment (GR 36/2021; mechanics in Permenaker 6/2016). Treat THR as an effectively **guaranteed 13th-month payroll cost**.

On expats, the RPTKA exemption is narrower than it looks: it applies **only to a director/commissioner who is also a qualifying shareholder** (the Investor-KITAS route above), and even then covers **only board-level governance, not operational work**. Everyone else - non-shareholder directors, below-threshold shareholders who want to work, and all operational/technical hires - needs **Ministry-of-Manpower RPTKA approval** within the eligible-role list (Kemnaker Decree 228/2019), with the **understudy/Bahasa obligations** and the **DKP-TKA levy**. **HR/personnel and industrial-relations roles are entirely closed to foreigners (Kepmenaker 349/2019).** Staffing a foreign-run operating team *below* board level is therefore genuinely hard - plan for a **local operating bench with a thin expat layer at board/advisory level**.

Overall, structure with mostly permanent local hires, disciplined performance management, and a lean expat footprint; budget severance and THR as standing liabilities.

## 7. Taxation

| Tax | Rate / rule |
|---|---|
| **Corporate Income Tax (CIT)** | **22%**; **19%** for listed companies with at least 40% free float meeting conditions |
| **SME regime** | **0.5% final tax** on turnover (PP 55/2022) - narrowed by GR 20/2026 (from 22 April 2026) to individuals, sole proprietorships, and cooperatives under IDR 4.8bn turnover; companies (PT/CV) no longer newly eligible. Separate 50% CIT discount tranche for turnover < IDR 50bn (about USD 2.8m) |
| **VAT (PPN)** | **12% statutory** from 1 Jan 2025 (PMK 131/2024), but **effective 11%** on most goods/services via an 11/12 taxable-base mechanism; full 12% only where luxury tax (PPnBM) applies. **PKP (VAT) registration threshold: IDR 4.8bn (about USD 267k)** turnover |
| **Withholding - payroll (PPh 21)** | Progressive; monthly TER tables (PP 58/2023) |
| **Withholding - domestic services (PPh 23)** | **2%** |
| **Withholding - non-resident (PPh 26)** | **20%** (or lower treaty rate) on dividends, interest, royalties, services |
| **Withholding - final (PPh 4(2))** | Various (rent, certain interest, etc.) |
| **E-commerce collection (PPh 22)** | **0.5%** withheld by qualifying marketplaces on domestic sellers' gross turnover, from **14 July 2025** (PMK 37/2025); individual sellers < IDR 500m (about USD 28k) turnover exempt on filing a declaration; corporate sellers not exempt |
| **Crypto** | PMK 50/2025 (from Aug 2025): crypto trades no longer VAT-able (treated like securities); **0.21% final** PPh for domestic platforms, 1% foreign |
| **Luxury goods (PPnBM)** | 10-200% on selected items |

**Financial-services specifics:**
- Core **financial and insurance services are largely VAT-exempt**; **loan interest is not VAT-able**.
- **P2P lender interest** is generally subject to **withholding (15% resident / 20% non-resident)** with the platform as collector (fintech tax rules, PMK 69/2022) - model this into product yields.
- **Coretax**, the new integrated tax-administration platform, went live in 2025 (with well-documented teething problems); build compliance ops accordingly.

**Cross-border / repatriation:** dividends paid abroad face **20% WHT**, reducible under an applicable double-tax treaty. **Verify the treaty position between Indonesia and the investor's home jurisdiction** (and any intermediate holding-company jurisdiction, e.g., Singapore) for the effective rate on dividends, interest, and capital gains before finalising an ownership chain - this materially affects net returns.

**Insider view:** headline rates are moderate and competitive. The friction is **compliance density** (monthly WHT/VAT filings, e-Faktur, Coretax) and **new digital-economy collection rules**, not the rates. A tax-efficient holding structure and strong local finance function matter more than rate arbitrage.
